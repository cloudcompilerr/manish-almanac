00 · The Three Pillars

Metrics, Logs, and Traces — why you need all three

The hospital analogy — for a complete layman
Your system is a patient. Observability is how doctors understand what is happening inside.

Metrics are the vital signs: heart rate 72bpm, blood pressure 120/80. Numbers over time. They tell you something is wrong but not why.

Logs are the doctor's notes: "14:23:01 — patient reported chest pain. 14:23:45 — administered aspirin." Text events at specific times. They tell you what happened, step by step.

Traces are the MRI scan: shows exactly which organ is causing the problem, how blood flows through each one, and where the blockage is. For distributed systems: shows exactly which service caused the latency.
Metrics
Numeric measurements over time. Aggregated, efficient, alertable.
Examples: requests/sec, p99 latency, error rate, CPU usage, DB connections.
Storage: time-series databases (Prometheus, InfluxDB) designed for fast aggregation over time ranges. ✓ Cheap to store (numbers, not text). Fast to query. Essential for dashboards and alerting. Answer: "is something wrong?" ⚠ Cannot tell you why something is wrong. High cardinality metrics (one time series per user ID) explode storage costs.
Logs
Text records of discrete events. Timestamped. Searchable.
Examples: "ERROR: DB connection failed — timeout after 5000ms", "INFO: user:123 logged in from IP 1.2.3.4"
Storage: Elasticsearch (ELK stack) or Loki (cheaper, label-based). ✓ Rich context. Exact error messages. Correlate events by user/session/request ID. Answer: "what exactly happened?" ⚠ Expensive to store at high volume. Unstructured logs are hard to query — use structured JSON logging. Do not log sensitive data.
Traces
Records the path of a single request through all microservices, with timing at each hop.
A user request spawns a trace with a unique trace_id. Each service call is a span. The trace shows: API Gateway (10ms) → Auth Service (5ms) → Product Service (200ms) → DB query (180ms of that 200ms). ✓ Immediately identifies which service is slow. Essential for microservices. Answer: "which service is causing the problem?" ⚠ Requires instrumentation in every service. Storage costs proportional to request volume × service count. Use sampling (store 1% of traces, all error traces).
The interview trap that catches most candidates

"We have Grafana dashboards for CPU, memory, and request rate" is monitoring, not observability — and interviewers who ask "how would you debug an issue you've never seen before?" are testing for the difference. Monitoring answers questions you thought to ask in advance (a dashboard for a known failure mode). Observability is the ability to ask a new question about system behaviour on the fly — "show me every request from user 4471 that touched the payments service and took >2s, broken down by which downstream call was slow" — without having pre-built a dashboard for that exact question. Candidates who describe observability purely as "we have lots of dashboards and alerts" are describing monitoring. The strong answer names high-cardinality, high-dimensionality data (traces and structured events you can slice on any field after the fact) as the thing that makes genuinely novel questions answerable.

01 · SLI, SLO, SLA

How to define and measure reliability — Director-level expected

SLI
(Service Level Indicator)
A quantitative measure of service behaviour. The thing you actually measure.
Examples: request success rate (% of requests returning non-5xx), p99 latency (99th percentile response time), availability (% of time service is up). SLIs are the raw measurements. Choose SLIs that directly reflect the user experience.
SLO
(Service Level Objective)
The target for your SLI. The internal goal your team commits to.
Examples: "99.9% of requests complete successfully" (three nines), "p99 latency ≤ 200ms", "availability ≥ 99.95% per month". ✓ SLOs drive engineering decisions. If you are burning your error budget, prioritise reliability work. If you are comfortably meeting SLOs, invest in features. 99.9% availability = 8.7 hours downtime/year. 99.99% = 52 minutes. 99.999% = 5 minutes. Know these numbers.
SLA
(Service Level Agreement)
The contractual commitment to customers, with financial penalties for breach.
AWS EC2 SLA: 99.99% availability. If violated: customer gets service credits. ⚠ SLA should be more conservative than your SLO. Your internal SLO is 99.95% so you have headroom before violating the 99.9% SLA you've contractually committed to customers.
Error Budget
The amount of unreliability you are allowed per period before violating your SLO.
SLO: 99.9% success rate over 30 days. That is 0.1% allowed failures = 43.2 minutes of downtime allowed per month.
If you deploy a bad release and burn 30 minutes of error budget, you have 13 minutes left that month. ✓ Error budgets make reliability discussions data-driven. "We cannot deploy this risky feature — we have only 5 minutes of error budget left this month." Google SRE invented this concept. It is the key metric for Director-level reliability conversations.
02 · Tools & Golden Signals

Prometheus, Grafana, Jaeger, and the four golden signals

Four Golden Signals
(Google SRE Book)
The four metrics that matter most for any service:
1. Latency: how long it takes to serve a request. Track p50, p95, p99 separately — averages hide outliers.
2. Traffic: demand on your system (requests/sec, messages/sec, transactions/sec).
3. Errors: rate of failed requests (5xx errors, exceptions, timeouts).
4. Saturation: how "full" your service is (CPU %, memory %, DB connection pool utilisation, queue depth). ✓ If you only monitor these four for every service, you will catch 90% of production incidents before users notice.
Prometheus + Grafana
The standard open-source metrics stack.
Prometheus: pulls metrics from /metrics endpoints every 15s. Stores as time-series. PromQL query language for aggregations.
Grafana: dashboards on top of Prometheus (and many other sources). Pre-built dashboards for Kubernetes, PostgreSQL, Redis, etc.
rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m]) — error rate over last 5 minutes. Used by: Grafana Labs (obviously), Uber, DigitalOcean, dozens of FAANG-adjacent companies. Kubernetes ships Prometheus-compatible metrics by default.
Distributed Tracing
(Jaeger / Zipkin)
OpenTelemetry is the emerging standard for instrumentation. One SDK, plugs into any backend (Jaeger, Zipkin, Tempo, Datadog).
Instrument your code once with OpenTelemetry SDK → export traces to Jaeger → visualise spans in the Jaeger UI.
trace_id propagated in headers: traceparent: 00-{trace_id}-{span_id}-01
All services that receive this header add their span to the same trace. ✓ OpenTelemetry is vendor-neutral — switch backends without re-instrumenting. Used by: Google Cloud Trace, AWS X-Ray, Datadog, New Relic all accept OTLP format.
Structured Logging
Log as JSON instead of plain text. Every field is queryable.
Bad: "ERROR: user 123 failed login from 1.2.3.4 at 14:23:01" — requires regex to parse.
Good: {"level":"error","event":"login_failed","user_id":123,"ip":"1.2.3.4","ts":"14:23:01"} — directly filterable in Elasticsearch or Loki. ✓ Filter all errors for user:123 in seconds. Correlate across services by request_id. Aggregate error types. Essential for production debugging at scale.

02b · Interview Decision Framework

What to instrument first — and how much observability a system actually needs

🎯Work Through These Before Proposing a Full Three-Pillar Stack
1
Can you currently answer "why" an incident happened, or only "what" happened?
Only "what" (metrics only) →Add structured logging next. Metrics tell you a spike happened; logs tell you which requests, users, or error types were involved. This is almost always the highest-leverage next investment for a system that only has dashboards.
Can answer "why" within one service →Move to step 2 — the gap is likely cross-service, not within a single service.
2
Does a single user request touch more than one service?
Yes, multiple services →Distributed tracing earns its keep here. The moment "which of these five services added the latency?" becomes a real question, metrics and logs alone force you to grep service-by-service. Tracing answers it in one view.
No, monolith or single service →Tracing has limited value yet — invest in better structured logging and in-process profiling instead. Full distributed tracing is premature instrumentation for a single-service system.
3
Do you have a defined SLO, or are alerts based on raw infrastructure thresholds?
Raw thresholds (CPU > 80%) →Define an SLO before adding more alerts. Alerting on infrastructure metrics without a user-facing SLO is the single biggest cause of alert fatigue — teams stop trusting pages and start ignoring them.
SLO-based burn-rate alerting →You're at the point where the remaining investment is tuning sampling and cardinality control, not adding more signal types.
03 · Debugging a Production Incident

The systematic approach — Director-level expected

The Incident Response Flow
A structured approach to diagnosing production issues in real time:
1. What is broken? → Check golden signals dashboard. Which service is elevated on errors or latency?
2. When did it start? → Correlate with recent deployments (deployment markers on Grafana). Was there a deploy 5 minutes before the spike?
3. Which requests are affected? → Sample error traces in Jaeger. Is it all users or a specific cohort (specific region, device, API version)?
4. Why is it failing? → Drill into traces to find the slow/failing span. Check logs for that service around that time.
5. Fix or rollback? → If recent deploy is culprit: rollback first, diagnose later. Never debug a production incident with a new deployment in flight.
Cardinality Problem
High-cardinality labels in metrics explode your time-series database cost and performance.
Bad: http_requests_total{user_id="12345", ...} — if 1M users, this creates 1M time series. Prometheus stores each separately. Query performance degrades, storage costs explode.
Good: http_requests_total{status="200", endpoint="/api/feed"} — low cardinality labels only. Use traces for per-user debugging, not metrics. ⚠ The rule: metrics are for aggregates (rates, percentiles, counts by cohort). Traces are for individual requests. Never put high-cardinality identifiers (user_id, request_id) in metric labels.
Sampling Strategy

Storing 100% of traces at high traffic is cost-prohibitive. Smart sampling is essential.

Head-based sampling: decide at trace start whether to sample. Simple (coin flip at 1%). Misses rare errors — a 0.01% error rate with 1% sampling = you only see 1 in 10,000 errors.

Tail-based sampling: collect all spans, decide at trace completion. Always sample: all traces with errors, all traces over p99 latency, 1% of normal traces. Captures all interesting events.

✓ Tail-based sampling is the production standard (Honeycomb, Datadog APM, OpenTelemetry Collector). Ensures no errors are missed while controlling storage costs.

05 · Alerting Best Practices

Building alerts that are actionable, not noisy

Symptom vs Cause
Alert on symptoms (user-visible problems), not causes (infrastructure metrics).
Bad: "Alert when CPU > 80%." CPU spikes happen constantly, usually resolve, rarely impact users.
Good: "Alert when p99 latency > 500ms for >2 minutes." This directly measures user experience.
The test: "If this alert fires at 3am, would I do something different immediately?" If no → it should be a ticket, not a page.
Multi-Window Alerting
Use two windows to reduce false positives: a short window for detection, a longer window for confirmation.
Alert if: error rate > 5% for last 2 minutes (fast detection) AND error rate > 1% for last 1 hour (confirms ongoing issue, not a spike).
Single-window alerts are noisy — a 30-second spike fires the alert. Multi-window only fires when the issue is sustained.
On-Call Runbook
Every alert must link to a runbook — documented steps the on-call engineer follows.
Runbook for "p99 latency elevated": (1) Check Grafana: which endpoint is slow? (2) Open Jaeger: find slow traces for that endpoint. (3) Is it a DB query? Check slow query log. (4) Is it an external API? Check circuit breaker status. (5) If new deploy is correlated: rollback procedure link. ✓ Reduces mean time to resolution (MTTR). New engineers can handle alerts without senior help. Runbooks should be updated after every incident — add what was missed.
03 · Follow-ups

Every hard follow-up, answered

How do you set meaningful SLOs — how do you know what 99.9% vs 99.99% means for your system?+
Start with what users actually notice, not an arbitrary number.

Step 1 — Measure current reliability: what is your actual success rate over the last 30 days? This is your baseline. Setting an SLO below current performance is pointless.

Step 2 — Understand user tolerance: what level of error do users actually experience and complain about? For a social feed, a 0.1% error rate might be imperceptible. For a payment API, even 0.01% errors (one in 10,000 payments failing) would cause serious complaints.

Step 3 — Cost of reliability: going from 99.9% to 99.99% availability typically requires: redundant infrastructure, geographic distribution, more complex failure handling. Is the engineering investment justified by the product need?

Typical starting points: User-facing read API: 99.9%. User-facing write API: 99.95%. Payment processing: 99.99%. Internal infrastructure services: 99.5%. Batch processing: 99%.
How do you avoid alert fatigue while still catching real incidents?+
Alert on symptoms, not causes. Alert on SLO burn rate, not on individual metric thresholds.

The anti-pattern: "Alert when CPU > 80%." CPU spikes happen constantly and usually resolve without user impact. Engineers learn to ignore the alerts (alert fatigue). When a real incident happens, it is buried in noise.

Better approach — SLO burn rate alerting: alert when you are burning your error budget faster than sustainable. "Alert when error rate over the last 1 hour would exhaust the monthly error budget in 2 days." This alert is high-signal — it means users are actually experiencing problems at a rate that will violate your SLO.

Alert design rules:
1. Every alert must be actionable — if there is no runbook for it, delete it
2. Alerts should page at most once per incident, not continuously
3. Alert on p99 latency, not p50 — averages hide the long tail that users experience
4. Distinguish page (wake someone up) vs ticket (fix in business hours) severity
How do you implement distributed tracing without instrumenting every microservice?+
Use service mesh sidecar proxies (Envoy/Istio) for automatic trace propagation without touching application code.

Sidecar-based tracing: Istio injects Envoy sidecar proxies alongside every service. Envoy automatically: generates trace IDs for new requests, propagates traceparent header to outbound calls, records span timing for every hop, exports spans to Jaeger/Zipkin/Tempo.

Application code does not need any instrumentation changes. You get traces for all service-to-service calls immediately.

Limitation: Envoy can only trace at the network boundary. Internal operations (DB queries, function calls within a service) require application-level instrumentation.

Hybrid approach: Envoy handles inter-service tracing automatically. Add OpenTelemetry SDK to only the highest-value services (payment, order creation) for internal DB query tracing. You get 80% of the value with 20% of the instrumentation effort.

Trace context propagation: the key is that the traceparent header (W3C Trace Context standard) must be forwarded by every service. Even without full SDK instrumentation, if services forward this header, trace IDs connect across the call graph.
How do you define SLOs for a complex system with multiple components?+
Define SLOs at the user-facing level, not at the component level. Users do not care which component failed.

Wrong approach: separate SLOs for Kafka (99.9%), Flink (99.5%), Redis (99.99%), ClickHouse (99.9%), API (99.95%). These cascade — if any one component hits its limit, the user-facing system fails.

Correct approach: one SLO for the user-observable service: "GET /top-k returns a successful response in <100ms for 99.9% of requests over any 30-day window."

Component SLIs feed the top-level SLO: if Flink falls behind → rankings get stale → API still returns 200 but with stale data. Is this an SLO violation? Depends on your SLI definition — include freshness in the SLI if staleness is user-visible.

The CUJ (Critical User Journey) approach from Google SRE: define SLOs per user journey, not per service. "User can see updated trending content within 60 seconds of an event going viral." This SLO captures the entire pipeline (Kafka lag + Flink latency + Redis update + API response) as a single observable outcome.

Start with 2–3 top-level SLOs per major user journey. Add component-level SLIs only when you need finer-grained alerting to diagnose which component is contributing to SLO burn.
08 · OpenTelemetry & Cost Control

The emerging standard and the economics of observability

01 OpenTelemetry Architecture
What it is
Vendor-neutral standard for collecting traces, metrics, and logs. The successor to proprietary SDKs.

Components: (1) SDK: instrument your code with otel-go, otel-java, otel-python etc. Auto-instrumentation: inject agent, zero code changes, frameworks instrumented automatically. (2) Collector: a daemon that receives telemetry from services, processes/filters/batches, and exports to backends. (3) Backend: Jaeger (traces), Prometheus (metrics), any OTLP-compatible backend (Datadog, Honeycomb, Grafana Tempo).

Why OTel matters: write instrumentation once → switch backends without code changes. Instrument once: the OTel SDK emits spans, metrics, AND logs through a unified API. Previously: separate libraries for each signal type from each vendor.

✓✓ Adopted by: Google Cloud, AWS, Microsoft Azure, Datadog (OTel ingest), Honeycomb, Grafana, New Relic. CNCF graduated project — this is the industry standard for the next decade.
02 Tail-Based Sampling
What it is
Sample based on what happened in the trace — not randomly at the start.

Head-based sampling (bad): decide at the first span whether to sample the trace. 1% sample rate → 99% of slow/error traces discarded — the ones you actually need.

Tail-based sampling (good): collect ALL spans. At the end of the trace, decide whether to keep it based on: did it have an error? Was the latency > 2s? Did it touch a critical service? Keep 100% of interesting traces, sample down boring fast-success traces to 1%.

Implementation: OTel Collector with tail sampling processor. Collector holds spans in memory for 30 seconds (waiting for the trace to complete), then applies the sampling policy. Cost: 30s × trace_rate × span_size of memory in the collector. Use a collector cluster with consistent hashing on trace_id (all spans of one trace must go to the same collector node).

03 Cardinality & Observability Costs
What it is
High-cardinality dimensions (user_id, order_id as metric labels) explode storage costs.

Prometheus with label user_id: 10M users × 5 metrics each = 50M time series. Prometheus struggles above 10M time series (RAM for TSDB head). Monthly storage: 50M series × 2 bytes/sample × 15s scrape = catastrophic.

The rule: metric labels must be low-cardinality (< 10,000 unique values). High-cardinality analysis belongs in traces (where each trace is a single row with any dimensions) or logs (Elasticsearch/Loki).

Cost-control levers: (1) Recording rules: pre-compute expensive aggregations in Prometheus, delete the raw series. (2) Remote write with downsampling: 1s resolution for 24h, 1min for 30 days, 1h for 1 year (Thanos/Cortex). (3) Label cardinality alerts: alert when a metric series count grows by 10× (indicates a cardinality explosion from a code change).

⚠ · Failure Modes

What actually breaks in observability systems — real incident patterns

01Cardinality Explosion Taking Down the TSDB Itself
What breaks & how
A code change adds a high-cardinality label — user_id, request_id, or a raw error message — to a metric. Each unique label value creates a new time series. Prometheus's in-memory head block grows unbounded, RAM usage climbs, and the Prometheus instance itself becomes slow or crashes — taking down the monitoring for the very incident it should be helping diagnose.
⚠Fix: enforce label cardinality budgets in code review (no unbounded-value labels on metrics — ever). Alert on time-series count growth (a metric growing 10× in series count is a strong signal of a cardinality bug in a recent deploy). Push anything genuinely per-request or per-user into traces or logs, not metric labels.
02Alert Fatigue Burying the Real Incident
What breaks & how
Alerts fire on raw infrastructure thresholds (CPU > 80%, disk > 70%) that spike constantly and self-resolve without user impact. On-call engineers learn to acknowledge and ignore pages. When a real, user-facing incident happens, its page looks identical to the hundred noise pages before it and gets the same half-attention — increasing time to detection and response for the incident that actually matters.
⚠Fix: alert on user-facing symptoms and SLO burn rate, not raw infrastructure thresholds. Every alert must have a linked runbook — if there's no action to take, it shouldn't page. Track and review alert-to-actionable-incident ratio; a page that's ignored more often than it's acted on is a bug in the alert, not the on-call engineer.
03The One Trace You Needed Wasn't Sampled
What breaks & how
With naive head-based sampling at a low rate (e.g. 1%), a rare but severe failure — a specific customer's request hitting an edge case — has a 99% chance of never being captured. During a post-incident investigation, the trace that would have shown exactly which downstream call failed simply doesn't exist, and the team is back to correlating logs by timestamp.
⚠Fix: use tail-based sampling so the decision to keep a trace happens after it's known whether the trace was slow or errored — always keep 100% of error and high-latency traces, sample down only the boring, fast, successful ones. This is standard practice in production observability platforms (OpenTelemetry Collector's tail-sampling processor, Honeycomb, Datadog APM).
📋 · Requirements & Scale

Requirements & Non-Functional Constraints

Functional Requirements

FR1The system must expose quantitative, aggregable health signals for every service (request rate, latency percentiles, error rate) so an on-call engineer can tell "is something wrong?" from a dashboard without reading logs.
FR2The system must record structured, timestamped events for individual occurrences (a specific request failing, a specific user's login) so an engineer can answer "what exactly happened?" after the fact, filterable by request/user/session id.
FR3The system must be able to reconstruct the path and per-hop timing of a single request as it crosses multiple services, so an engineer can identify which specific service or call added the latency or threw the error, not just that the overall request was slow.
FR4The system must page a human only when a user-facing symptom crosses a threshold that actually requires action, with a linked runbook — not on every transient infrastructure metric spike — to keep the signal-to-noise ratio high enough that on-call engineers keep trusting pages.
FR5The system must give on-call engineers a real-time view of current system health without requiring them to already know which dashboard to open — golden-signal dashboards per service, reachable within seconds of a page firing.
FR6The system must bound its own storage and ingestion cost as traffic grows, via sampling (traces), retention tiering (metrics), and label-cardinality discipline — observability infrastructure should never become the largest line item in the infra budget by accident.

Non-Functional Requirements

NFR1Alert-to-page latency is a target you set based on your alert-evaluation interval and routing pipeline (e.g. Prometheus evaluates rules on a fixed interval, then AlertManager routes) — state the interval you're designing for and derive the end-to-end number from it, rather than asserting a fixed "2 minutes" without doing that arithmetic.
NFR2Metrics retention tiering (high resolution for a short window, downsampled for longer windows) is a design choice to control storage cost — a commonly used pattern is full resolution for a day or two, downsampled to minutes for a month, and to hours for a year (this is what Thanos/Cortex downsampling is built for), but the specific numbers are yours to justify against your query needs and budget, not a spec to cite.
NFR3Tail-based sampling — keep 100% of error and high-latency traces, sample down the rest — is the production-standard strategy; the sampling percentage for "normal" traffic (this doc uses 1% as an illustrative target) is a cost/fidelity trade-off you tune against actual trace volume and storage budget.
NFR4Log ingestion volume is a direct function of request volume × log verbosity — compute your own figure from expected QPS and average log-line size rather than assuming a fixed "1TB/day"; whatever the number, structured JSON logs into Loki/Elasticsearch (hot) with S3+Athena archive (cold, compliance-retention) is the standard split.
NFR5Prometheus's own documentation is stricter than most engineers assume: its stated rule of thumb is to avoid any single metric whose label cardinality could grow past roughly 10, and to have at most a handful that reach the low hundreds — not thousands. A "keep it under 10,000 unique values per label" ceiling is sometimes used informally as an outer emergency bound, not an endorsed target; design for the smallest cardinality that answers your actual questions and push anything higher-cardinality into traces or logs instead.
NFR6Error-budget arithmetic depends on which month length you use: 99.9% over a calendar 30-day month is 43.2 minutes of allowed downtime (0.1% × 30 × 1440 min); over the average 30.44-day month it's 43.8 minutes. Both are "correct" — state which basis you're using rather than quoting one figure as if it were universal.

What To Instrument First

SituationHighest-leverage next investment
You only have infrastructure dashboardsStructured logging — answers "why," not just "what"
A single request touches multiple servicesDistributed tracing — pinpoints which hop added latency
Alerts fire on raw thresholds (CPU, disk)Define an SLO and switch to burn-rate alerting

Failure Mode Fast Recall

SymptomLikely causeFix
Prometheus itself goes slow or falls overCardinality explosion — a label grew an unbounded number of unique valuesEnforce label-cardinality budgets in review; alert on time-series count growth; move per-request/per-user data to traces or logs
On-call stops reacting to pages, misses a real incidentAlert fatigue from thresholding raw infra metrics instead of user-facing symptomsAlert on SLO burn rate; require a runbook per alert; track page-to-actionable-incident ratio
The one trace you needed during an investigation doesn't existNaive head-based sampling discarded it before anyone knew it would matterSwitch to tail-based sampling — always keep 100% of error/high-latency traces

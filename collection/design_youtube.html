00 · Requirements

Requirements & Scale Assumptions

Functional

FR1A creator must be able to upload a large video file from an unreliable connection and have the upload survive interruption, resuming from where it left off rather than restarting from byte zero.
FR2Every uploaded video must be converted into multiple resolution/bitrate variants so viewers on a wide range of devices and connection speeds can each get a version suited to their situation, not one-size-fits-all.
FR3A viewer's player must be able to switch video quality up or down mid-playback as their network conditions change, without the video stopping or restarting.
FR4A viewer must be able to find a video by matching text against its title, description, or spoken content, not only by already knowing its exact title.
FR5Viewers must be able to see aggregate engagement (views, likes, comments) on a video, and post their own comments, without every view needing a synchronous write to a durable store.
FR6The system must surface videos a given viewer is likely to want to watch next, based on their own and similar viewers' behavior, rather than requiring them to search for everything manually.

Non-Functional

NFR1500 hours of video uploaded per minute (YouTube's own publicly stated figure, cited consistently since 2019 and still the figure in current use as of this writing).
NFR2~2.5B+ monthly active users (2024–2025 third-party estimates cluster around 2.5–2.7B; YouTube doesn't publish an exact figure), 1B+ hours watched per day (a figure Google/YouTube has stated publicly since 2017 and reconfirmed as of 2024) — treat both as sizing inputs, the first an estimate and the second a disclosed floor.
NFR3Design target: video start time under 2 seconds — a target to justify from the playback pipeline (small first segment, warm CDN cache), not a disclosed YouTube SLA.
NFR4Design target: 99.99% availability — a standard SLA tier to propose and justify, not a disclosed YouTube number.
NFR5Design target: under ~50ms to fetch a video chunk globally — justified by edge caching putting content physically close to viewers, not a disclosed metric.
NFR6Storage: exabyte-scale is the honest characterization — YouTube/Google has never published a specific PB figure for YouTube's corpus, so any precise number here (including this exercise's own back-of-envelope estimate below) is an illustrative capacity-estimation exercise, not a disclosed fact.

The TV broadcast analogy
Old TV: one signal broadcast from one tower, everyone watches the same thing at the same time. YouTube: millions of people each watching a different video, pausing, rewinding, choosing quality — and 500 hours of new content added every minute. The magic: every video is sliced into 2-second chunks (HLS segments). When you "stream," your device is actually downloading a sequence of tiny files, each cached at a nearby server (CDN PoP). Seeking to 45:30 = requesting chunk #1350. The CDN probably already has it — so it's instant. The transcoding farm converts each upload into 5 quality levels in parallel, so 360p is ready in 5 minutes while 4K takes 90.
⚠ The interview trap that catches most candidates

"Design YouTube" candidates who say "store the video file and stream it from S3/GCS" are describing progressive download, not adaptive streaming — and it falls apart in two ways an interviewer will immediately probe. First, seeking: a single unsegmented file forces either downloading from the start or an HTTP Range request to an arbitrary byte offset, which a CDN caches terribly (every unique seek position is a distinct, uncacheable byte range). Second, quality: a single file is a single bitrate — there's no way to drop from 1080p to 360p mid-playback when a viewer's connection degrades on a train. The actual hard problem is two decisions working together: segment the video into small independently-fetchable chunks (HLS/DASH), and encode multiple bitrate/resolution variants of every segment so the player can switch between them without interrupting playback. Naming "adaptive bitrate streaming" as one integrated decision — not "compression" and "seeking" as two separate afterthoughts — is what signals real understanding here.

01 · Architecture

Full System — Upload, Transcode, Deliver

01Resumable Upload PipelineGCS Multipart + Pub/Sub
Upload Flow
Client splits video into 8MB chunks. Upload chunks in parallel. Resume on failure.
1. Client: POST /upload/initiate → server creates an upload session (session_id, GCS resumable upload URL). 2. Client splits file: chunk_1.bin, chunk_2.bin, ... with SHA-256 hash per chunk. 3. Upload up to 5 chunks in parallel directly to GCS (pre-signed URLs). Server never handles video bytes. 4. On failure: client queries GET /upload/{session_id}/status → server queries GCS for received bytes → client resumes from the first missing chunk. 5. On completion: GCS triggers Pub/Sub event → transcoding pipeline starts.
✓Pre-signed URL upload: your API servers handle only metadata (kilobytes). GCS handles the gigabytes. 500 simultaneous 8GB uploads = 4TB of traffic that never touches your servers
ℹtus.io is the open standard for resumable uploads. YouTube uses a similar proprietary protocol. The key: each chunk has a checksum; the server verifies before acknowledging
02Transcoding PipelineDistributed Encoder Farm
Encoding Strategy
Source video → parallel encode into 5 quality variants + thumbnails + audio + subtitles.
GCS upload complete → Pub/Sub event → Transcoding Orchestrator creates a job: {source_path, video_id, target_resolutions: [360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K]}. Job split into parallel tasks: each resolution encoded independently on GPU instances. H.264 for broad compatibility, VP9 for Chrome (20% smaller files). Encoder: FFmpeg on g4dn.xlarge (NVIDIA T4 GPU).
Also generates: 1 thumbnail every 10 seconds (for scrubbing preview), audio track (AAC), auto-captions (speech-to-text → WebVTT).
✓Parallel encoding: 4K encode of 1-hour video takes 90 min on 1 GPU. With 5 resolutions in parallel: total wall time is the slowest (4K) = 90 min. Sequential would be 90+45+20+10+5 = 170 min
→360p available first (fastest to encode): video goes live in ~5 minutes for a 10-minute video. 4K unlocks an hour later. This is exactly YouTube's behaviour
03HLS Adaptive Streaming2-Second Segments + Manifest
Streaming Protocol
Video split into 2-second .ts segments. Client downloads segments sequentially, adapting quality based on bandwidth.
Each quality variant has a manifest file (720p.m3u8) listing all segment URLs: seg_001.ts, seg_002.ts, ... A master manifest lists all variant manifests. Player: download master manifest → choose quality based on measured bandwidth → download segments ahead (2-second buffer).
Bandwidth drops: player switches to lower manifest (360p). Seeks: player calculates which segment contains the target timestamp → fetch that segment directly from CDN. O(1) seek regardless of video length.
✓2-second segments means any seek requires downloading only 2 seconds of video before playback resumes. No server-side seek operations — pure client-side math
ℹDASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP) is the alternative — used by Netflix. Functionally similar to HLS. YouTube uses HLS for Apple devices, DASH for Android/Chrome
04CDN ArchitectureGoogle's Own Edge Network
Global Delivery
Google CDN co-located in ISPs worldwide. Popular videos pre-pushed to ISP cache servers.
At 1.4 Tbps sustained outbound: no public CDN (Cloudflare, CloudFront) can serve this cost-effectively. Google operates their own edge caching infrastructure inside ISPs (same model as Netflix Open Connect). User in Mumbai watches "Gangnam Style" → served from the Jio edge node in the same building as Jio's own servers → < 5ms latency, zero long-distance transit cost.
Cache strategy: pre-push top 1% of videos (by view count) to all edge nodes. Long-tail (99%) served from regional GCS bucket via CDN on-demand cache.
⚠Cache invalidation: deleted or copyright-struck videos must be purged from all edge nodes. Google CDN propagates purges globally in < 60 seconds via its Anycast infrastructure
→Range requests: HTTP Range header enables seeking into a cached video segment without re-downloading from start. CDN must support byte-range requests (all major CDNs do)
05View CountRedis Write-Behind + BigQuery
Analytics
Redis INCR for live counter. BigQuery for historical analytics. Never hit PostgreSQL per view.
View event: INCR view:count:{video_id}. Return new count to client. Background job every 60s: for each changed key, GETSET view:count:{video_id} 0 → append (video_id, delta, timestamp) to BigQuery streaming insert. BigQuery: analytics queries (views by country, device, cohort).
Display: for hot videos (< 24h old): read from Redis (fresh). For cold videos: read from BigQuery materialized view (updated every 5 min).
ℹYouTube famously froze view counts at exactly 301 views during manual anti-fraud review. This is now automated: ML model flags suspicious view patterns (same IPs, no engagement, bot-like timing).
02 · Deep Dive

Upload to Live — Complete Lifecycle

Video goes from upload to watchable
T+0
Creator uploads a 10-minute 1080p video (2GB). Client initiates upload: POST /upload/initiate → {session_id, upload_url}. Video split into 256 × 8MB chunks.
T+0 to T+180s
5 chunks upload in parallel to GCS. Upload completes in ~3 minutes on 50Mbps connection. GCS confirms all chunks received and assembled.
T+181s
GCS publishes UploadComplete event to Pub/Sub. Transcoding Orchestrator picks it up. Creates 5 encoding jobs (one per resolution).
T+185s
5 GPU instances begin parallel encoding. 360p job is fastest — starts producing .ts segments within 30 seconds.
T+215s
360p encoding complete (30s for a 10-min video at low resolution). Segments uploaded to GCS. Manifest (360p.m3u8) generated. Video marked as "available at 360p."
T+600s
1080p encoding complete (~7 min). Master manifest updated to include 1080p variant. Viewer who started watching at 360p sees quality option appear.
T+5400s
4K variant complete (~90 min for 10-min video). Full quality unlocked. Video now available at all 5 resolutions globally via CDN.
03 · The Hard Decisions

Transcoding, Adaptive Bitrate, and CDN Strategy — Term by Term

Three decisions, made together, are what actually make "streaming video to billions of people" tractable. Get the vocabulary precise.

PARALLEL
TRANSCODING

One uploaded source file is independently re-encoded into several resolution/bitrate variants (e.g. 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K) in parallel, not in sequence. Because each variant is encoded by a separate job, the wall-clock time to "video is live" is bounded by the slowest single variant (usually 4K), not the sum of all of them — and the lowest-resolution variant, which finishes fastest, can go live first while higher resolutions are still processing.

SEGMENTATION
(HLS/DASH)

Each encoded variant is further sliced into short (commonly ~2–10 second) independently-fetchable segments, indexed by a manifest file. This is the piece that makes both seeking and CDN caching work: seeking to a timestamp is just "compute which segment number that falls in and fetch it" (O(1), no server-side seek logic), and a CDN can cache each segment as its own immutable object rather than trying to cache arbitrary byte ranges of one giant file.

ADAPTIVE
BITRATE (ABR)

The player continuously measures its own effective download throughput and switches between variant manifests in real time — up in quality when bandwidth is healthy, down when it degrades — without interrupting playback. Because every variant is segmented at the same time boundaries, the player can switch from the 1080p manifest to the 360p manifest between any two segments with no gap. This is the mechanism, not a magic "detect bandwidth" black box — it only works because segmentation made every variant swappable at the same cut points.

HLS (Apple-originated, Apple-device-native) and MPEG-DASH (the open, codec-agnostic standard used heavily by Android/Chrome and by Netflix) solve the same problem with different manifest formats — functionally comparable, and it's common for a single platform to serve both depending on client.
CDN
PRE-POSITIONING

Segments of popular content are pushed to edge caches — sometimes physically inside ISP networks — before demand arrives, rather than waiting for the first request to trigger a cache fill. At YouTube's outbound bandwidth (routinely cited around a terabit-plus, sustained, from ~1B+ hours watched/day), transiting a public CDN for every byte would be cost-prohibitive; embedding cache infrastructure inside ISPs is the same strategy Netflix documents publicly for its own Open Connect program. The payoff: the byte a viewer receives usually never leaves their own ISP's network.

04 · Interview Decision Framework

How Many Variants, How Short a Segment, How Aggressive a Cache?

🎯Four Questions That Decide the Streaming Design
1
Is time-to-first-frame or eventual quality the more important metric for this product?
Time-to-first-frame (short-form, high-churn viewing) →Publish the lowest-resolution variant the moment it finishes encoding and let higher resolutions unlock progressively — viewers start watching in minutes, not after the full transcode pipeline completes.
Guaranteed full-quality availability before publish (premium/licensed content) →Wait for all variants to finish before making the video visible at all — simpler consistency story, at the cost of publish latency.
2
How latency-sensitive is the content — pre-recorded VOD, or live?
VOD →Longer segments (~6–10s) are fine — fewer manifest entries and HTTP requests per hour of viewing, and startup/seek latency is a secondary concern.
Live →Shorter segments (~2s) reduce end-to-end glass-to-glass latency at the cost of more overhead per hour of content — the standard trade in live-streaming protocol design.
3
Does the catalog have a small "hot" set that accounts for most views, or is viewing spread evenly across a huge long tail?
Heavy hot/cold skew (typical of video platforms) →Pre-position the hot set at every edge node; serve the long tail on-demand from a regional cache-on-miss tier. Pre-positioning everything everywhere wastes edge storage on content nobody near that edge will watch.
Evenly spread →A simpler pure cache-on-miss CDN (no pre-positioning logic) captures most of the benefit with far less operational complexity.
4
Is GPU transcoding cost or transcoding speed the binding constraint?
Cost-bound (large archive, non-urgent backlog) →CPU-based batch transcoding is cheaper per hour of video processed, at the cost of much longer processing time — acceptable for content that isn't time-sensitive.
Speed-bound (new uploads must go live quickly) →GPU-accelerated parallel transcoding is worth its higher per-hour cost specifically because publish latency is user-facing.
⚠ · Failure Modes

What Actually Breaks — Production Failure Patterns

01Transcode Queue Backlog During an Upload Spike
What it is

A predictable event (a major sports final, a viral moment prompting a wave of reaction-video uploads) spikes upload volume well above steady-state. If the GPU transcoding fleet doesn't autoscale fast enough, the job queue backs up — creators see "processing" for far longer than usual, and time-to-live for new uploads silently degrades platform-wide, not just for the spike's own videos.

✓Fix: autoscale the transcoding fleet on queue depth (not a fixed pool size); prioritize the lowest-resolution variant of every job first across the whole queue so every video gets to "watchable" before any video gets to "highest quality," rather than processing videos strictly first-in-first-out end-to-end.
02Cold CDN Edge on a Surprise Viral Video
What it is

Pre-positioning only works for predictable virality (scheduled premieres, known creators with large followings). A video that goes unexpectedly viral organically hasn't been pre-pushed anywhere — the first wave of viewers at each edge PoP all cache-miss simultaneously and hit origin storage at the same time, right as the load is spiking hardest.

✓Fix: request coalescing at the edge (the first miss for a segment fetches from origin; concurrent misses for the same segment wait on that one in-flight fetch instead of each hitting origin independently); origin storage sized and rate-limited to survive a "every edge PoP misses at once" worst case, since it will eventually happen to some video.
03Stale Manifest After a Takedown
What it is

A video is removed (copyright strike, policy violation, creator deletion) but its manifest and segments remain cached at edge nodes that haven't yet received the purge. A viewer with a cached manifest URL can keep watching a video that's supposedly been taken down, for as long as the CDN's purge propagation takes.

✓Fix: purges need to propagate through the CDN's invalidation mechanism (not just deleted at origin) before a takedown is considered complete; the manifest itself should be short-TTL or re-validated frequently even though segments are long-TTL, since the manifest is the single choke point that can stop new segment fetches immediately.

07 · Hard Follow-ups

Every Question Your Interviewer Will Ask

How does video seeking work — how does a player jump to 45:30 in a 2-hour movie instantly?+
HLS segmentation makes seeking O(1). Each segment is an independent file.

The master manifest and each quality manifest contain the start time and URL of every segment. To seek to 45:30: player calculates 45:30 ÷ 2 seconds = segment index 1350. Fetch seg_1350.ts directly from CDN. Player discards its buffer, starts downloading from seg_1350.

The CDN has likely cached this segment (popular videos, popular scrub points). If not: CDN fetches from GCS origin (50ms). Either way: player has 2 seconds of video in < 100ms. Seek appears instantaneous.

Compare to a non-segmented MP4: seek would require HTTP Range request starting at the byte offset for 45:30. CDN cannot cache byte ranges efficiently — every unique seek position is a cache miss. HLS eliminates this problem by making each time slice a discrete cacheable file.
How do you detect copyright infringement — someone uploads a movie they don't own?+
Content ID: generate an audio and video fingerprint of every upload, match against a database of reference works submitted by rights holders.

YouTube states its Content ID database holds more than 50 million active reference files (some public reporting describes the corpus as representing 600+ years of audio/visual reference content) — not the much larger "800M" figure sometimes quoted, which doesn't appear in YouTube's own disclosures. During transcoding: extract audio track → generate an acoustic fingerprint (YouTube's own algorithm is proprietary and undisclosed; conceptually similar to open audio-fingerprinting approaches like Chromaprint, which represents the frequency spectrum over time compactly enough for fast approximate matching). Also: extract frame hashes for video fingerprinting (handles screen recordings, re-encodings).

Content ID database at ~50M reference files: even at a generous few hundred bytes per fingerprint, the index is small enough to serve from RAM or a fast ANN index (FAISS-style) for approximate nearest-neighbour lookup at upload time.

On match: copyright owner is notified. They choose: block the video, monetise it (ads, revenue share), or allow it. YouTube has stated Content ID handles the overwhelming majority of copyright claims on the platform this way — the exact per-day match volume isn't a figure YouTube publishes, so treat any specific number here as an estimate. The system is automated — no human review for clear matches.

Timing: Content ID check runs during transcoding. Video goes live only after Content ID clears (< 5 minutes for audio check). This is why there's a processing delay after upload.
How do you handle a video that goes viral — 100M views in the first hour?+
The CDN absorbs the spike. Your origin infrastructure sees at most cache-miss traffic.

A video going viral: first 1M viewers → CDN cache miss → GCS serves the first request per CDN PoP → each PoP caches the segments. 2nd through 100M viewers at the same PoP: CDN cache hit → zero GCS requests.

Global CDN: 200+ PoPs. For a video with 100M views/hour globally: at most 200 cache-miss requests to GCS (one per PoP). GCS: trivially handles 200 requests. CDN handles 100M requests independently.

The failure mode: if a video somehow bypasses CDN (direct GCS URL): 100M requests × 50ms GCS latency = catastrophic. Every video URL must go through CDN — enforced at the URL generation layer.

Pre-warming: for known events (World Cup final, Beyoncé concert drop), pre-push the video to all CDN PoPs before release. CDN cache is warm — first viewer gets the same experience as the millionth.
How does YouTube's recommendation system work at a high level?+
Two-stage: candidate generation → ranking. Billions of videos → hundreds of candidates → top 25 shown.

Stage 1 — Candidate Generation: for each user, generate ~500 candidate videos from: (a) collaborative filtering (users similar to you watched these — matrix factorisation on watch history), (b) content-based (similar to videos you watched — video embedding similarity), (c) freshness injection (recent uploads from subscribed channels). Computed using approximate nearest-neighbour search in embedding space. ~50ms.

Stage 2 — Ranking: deep neural network scores each candidate on: predicted watch time (not clicks — click-bait optimisation caused the "recommendation problem"), satisfaction signals (likes, shares, surveys), freshness, diversity. Top 25 returned. ~100ms on GPU.

The recommendation problem: optimising purely for click-through → click-bait thumbnails win. YouTube has publicly described shifting its ranking away from pure click-through toward watch time and, later, explicit satisfaction signals (surveys) over the course of the 2010s — the exact timeline of each change isn't something to state as a precise year with confidence. This reduced click-bait but introduced the "rabbit hole" problem (increasingly extreme content maximises watch time), which YouTube has also publicly acknowledged working against. Ongoing tuning.
